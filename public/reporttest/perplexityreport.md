# UK AI Digital Inclusion: Market Feasibility Report
**Is there a commercially viable business helping non-technical UK adults engage with AI?**

---

## 1. Executive Verdict

> **Verdict: There is a real, large, and growing pain — but it is mostly a poorly-funded social problem, not yet a healthy consumer market. A commercially viable business is possible, but only with a B2B2C or institution-paid model. Direct-to-consumer monetisation of the most-excluded adults is the weakest path and should be largely avoided.**

**Is this demographic worth building a business around?** Yes, on size; cautiously, on profitability. The underlying need is enormous and structurally durable. **15% of UK adults — 7.9 million people — lack Foundation Level digital skills**, **1.6 million adults are fully offline**, and **10.2 million UK adults lack digital skills overall**, according to the [Lloyds Bank Consumer Digital Index 2024](https://www.lloydsbankinggroup.com/assets/pdfs/media/consumer-digital-index/2024-consumer-digital-index-report.pdf). At the same time AI adoption is racing ahead of the excluded: **54% of UK adults now use AI tools, up from 31% in 2024**, per [Ofcom's 2026 media use research reported by the BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cgqk718l4neo), which means the confidence gap is widening, not closing. The demographic is worth serving. The hard question is who pays, because the people with the deepest need — **12.1 million in the "Very Low" digital capability group, of whom 54% are aged 70+ and 41% earn under £20,000/year** ([Lloyds CDI 2024](https://www.lloydsbankinggroup.com/assets/pdfs/media/consumer-digital-index/2024-consumer-digital-index-report.pdf)) — have the lowest ability and willingness to pay.

**Who is the best buyer?** Not the end user. The best buyers, in order, are: (1) **employers** — the [AI Opportunities Action Plan](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-opportunities-action-plan-dashboard/ai-opportunities-action-plan-infographic-accessible) targets **10 million upskilled workers by 2030** and employers spend roughly **£1,700 per trained employee** annually ([Employer Skills Survey analysis](https://learnexperts.ai/blog/how-much-do-companies-spend-on-training-per-employee/)); (2) **adult children / families** paying on behalf of older relatives, who can spend without being budget-constrained themselves; (3) **councils, libraries and charities** with grant funding such as the **£7.278 million Digital Inclusion Innovation Fund** and the **£9.5 million Digital Inclusion First Steps fund** ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/digital-inclusion-innovation-fund/digital-inclusion-innovation-fund)); and (4) **telecoms and banks** running large CSR programmes. The individual most-excluded adult should generally be a *sponsored* user, not a paying customer.

**How should the offer be framed?** Not as "AI." Reluctant, low-confidence adults are not buying a technology category — **38% told Ofcom they were "not interested" in generative AI and 35% said they "did not need to"** ([Ofcom Online Nation via The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/11/29/ofcom_online_nation/)). The strongest framings are **scam/fraud safety** (visceral, urgent, trusted), **life admin and saving money** (NHS appointments, benefits, bills, forms), and **job help** (for working-age cohorts). "Digital confidence" works as an umbrella; "AI" is the engine under the bonnet, not the badge on the bonnet.

**The single strongest wedge:** A **scam-safety-led "digital confidence" programme delivered to older adults and vulnerable people, paid for by institutions** (councils, housing associations, banks, telecoms) and **families**, with AI as the practical tool — using a familiar channel (phone/WhatsApp plus in-person), not an app the user must install and master. Scam fear is the single most powerful motivator in this market: **nearly 3 in 5 older people have been targeted by financial fraud and 1.9 million have been scammed, averaging £4,000 each — roughly £7.4 billion in total** ([Independent Age](https://www.independentage.org/sites/default/files/2024-10/The_hidden_cost_of_scams_Independent_Age.pdf)).

**What should be avoided:** (1) A paid consumer app requiring the excluded user to self-onboard — the cohort that **prefers face-to-face learning (64% of those with low internet confidence)** ([Lloyds CDI 2024](https://www.lloydsbankinggroup.com/assets/pdfs/media/consumer-digital-index/2024-consumer-digital-index-report.pdf)) will not self-serve. (2) Selling "AI training" to reluctant low-tech consumers at a premium price. (3) Competing head-on with free, trusted, well-funded incumbents (Good Things Foundation, Barclays, BT/AbilityNet, Age UK) on basic digital skills. (4) Care-home-resident-pays models — too frail, too safeguarded, too low-margin.

**Key sizing caveat (brutally honest):** The gap between the **30m+ non-AI-users** and the **~£95k base-case year-1 revenue** is the entire problem with this market. Need is colossal; *monetisable, low-friction* demand is thin. Revenue is gated by institutional budget cycles and grant availability, not by the size of the underlying population. The market is "big TAM, small SAM, tiny SOM."

## 4. Existing Spending on Digital Inclusion and AI Skills

The institutional money in this space is real but fragmented across government, education, employers, charities and corporate CSR. The pools below are the demand-side budgets a new entrant could capture.

### 4.1 Government / public-sector spending

- **Digital Inclusion Innovation Fund: £7.278 million** (2025), with grants of **£25,000–£500,000 per project** for charities and councils in England ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/digital-inclusion-innovation-fund/digital-inclusion-innovation-fund)).
- **Digital Inclusion First Steps fund: £9.5 million** for digital inclusion, announced alongside the action plan, with Essential Digital Skills fully funded for adults with low skills ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/digital-inclusion-action-plan-first-steps/digital-inclusion-action-plan-first-steps)).
- **DSIT Flexible AI Upskilling Fund: £7.4 million pilot** for SMEs in professional services, subsidising up to **50% of AI training costs (max £10,000 per medium business)** — though applications closed August 2024 ([DSIT guide](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/663108a4e6646073034b9bef/flexible-ai-upskilling-fund-pilot-application-guide.pdf)).
- **Total identified ring-fenced public spend on digital inclusion is only ~£24 million in the 2025–26 round** (author's sum of the three funds above) — strikingly small against a problem affecting tens of millions.

### 4.2 Adult education and skills budget (digital component)

- The **Adult Education Budget / Adult Skills Fund totals ~£1.455 billion (2024–25)** per the [National Audit Office](https://www.nao.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Department-for-Education-Overview-2024-25.pdf), with the **London Adult Skills Fund at £345.3 million**.
- **Essential Digital Skills Qualifications (EDSQ) are fully funded for adults 19+ with low digital skills**, and the **Free Courses for Jobs** programme includes digital skills ([NAO](https://www.nao.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Department-for-Education-Overview-2024-25.pdf)).
- **Skills Bootcamps** (funded via AEB) already finance AI bootcamps for eligible adults — e.g. the [Creative Process Digital AI for Business Bootcamp](https://creativeprocessdigital.com/courses/bootcamps/ai-for-business/), an 8-week course with a **funded value of £2,250**, free to eligible learners.
- The digital slice of the AEB is not separately published; as an **inference**, even a low-single-digit percentage of £1.455bn implies tens of millions annually flowing through accredited providers — the single largest accessible public pool, but only via provider status or subcontracting.

### 4.3 Employer training budgets for digital/AI skills

- UK employers spend roughly **£1,700 per employee trained in 2024 (down from £1,960 in 2022)**, or **£2,710 per person trained**, per the [2024 Employer Skills Survey analysis](https://learnexperts.ai/blog/how-much-do-companies-spend-on-training-per-employee/).
- Benchmark AI-specific budgets run **$1,000–$1,500 per employee per year** for comprehensive programmes (including tools) or **$400–$600 for delivery only**, per [Pertama Partners](https://www.pertamapartners.com/insights/cost-per-employee-ai-training-budgeting).
- The [AI Opportunities Action Plan](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-opportunities-action-plan-dashboard/ai-opportunities-action-plan-infographic-accessible) reports **1 million AI courses delivered since June 2025** against a **target of 10 million workers by 2030**, with **£68 billion of private investment pledged since January 2025** — a strong tailwind for employer-paid demand.

### 4.4 Charitable / grant funding for digital inclusion

- **Good Things Foundation** operates a **5,549-member National Digital Inclusion Network** and has **supported 598,000 people since 2022 (target 1 million by 2025)**, funded by grants, government contracts and corporate CSR ([Good Things Foundation](https://www.goodthingsfoundation.org/policy-and-research/research-and-evidence/research-2024/digital-nation)).
- The economic prize is well-evidenced: improving digital skills is valued at **£13.7bn for life and £23bn for work** ([Good Things Foundation](https://www.goodthingsfoundation.org/policy-and-research/research-and-evidence/research-2024/digital-nation)); an older cost-benefit study put the **NPV of full investment at £14.3bn with a 9.7:1 benefit ratio**, at a **cost of £44–£351 per learner** ([techUK/Good Things economic impact study](https://www.techskills.org/globalassets/pdfs/research-2015/economicimpactofdigitalskillsandinclusionintheuk_nov15.pdf)).

### 4.5 Telecom / bank CSR programmes

- **BT funds AbilityNet** to deliver free sessions for 65+ and disabled adults — **Phase 1 reached 5,063 people across 3,495 sessions, 95% rated excellent/good** ([AbilityNet](https://abilitynet.org.uk/sites/abilitynet.org.uk/files/BT-Digital-Skills-Impact-Report_Phase1-Full.pdf)).
- **Barclays Digital Eagles** delivers free in-branch sessions plus Eagle Labs for SMEs as CSR ([FutureDotNow directory](https://futuredotnow.uk/directory/digital-eagles-and-digital-wings-programme/)).
- **Virgin Media O2** has **connected 350,000+ digitally excluded people (target 1 million by end 2025)** and **Three's Discovery programme has reached 235,791 of a 270,000 target** ([Digital Inclusion Action Plan first steps](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/digital-inclusion-action-plan-first-steps/digital-inclusion-action-plan-first-steps); [one-year-on report](https://dera.ioe.ac.uk/id/eprint/42064/1/Digital%20inclusion%20action%20plan%20One%20year%20on%20-%20GOV_Redacted.pdf)).
- These are the most likely **paying sponsors** for a new AI-specific layer that the incumbents do not yet provide.

### 4.6 Consumer direct spending on tech support / tuition

- Charitable/local provision is priced near-free: **Age Concern Epsom suggests a £20/session donation** for home visits ([Age Concern Epsom](https://ageconcernepsom.org.uk/our-services/it-support/)); **Superprof private tutors average ~£20/hour** in London ([Superprof](https://www.superprof.co.uk/private-tech-lessons-for-older-adults-phones-email-zoom-more.html)); **TechCares charges £50/month per 2-hour session** for individuals ([TechCares](https://techcares.co.uk/Home/price)).
- This sets a hard **consumer price ceiling of roughly £20–£50 per session**, dragged down by free substitutes — a critical constraint on any D2C model.

### 4.7 B2B AI training market

- Boutique specialist half-day workshops run **£980–£1,950 per session**, freelance trainers **£400–£900 half-day**, and enterprise L&D providers **£395–£795 per person**, per [LogicRoad's 2026 UK benchmarks](https://logicroad.co.uk/blog/how-much-does-on-site-ai-training-cost-uk-2026).
- Comparable providers: [LogicRoad](https://logicroad.co.uk/blog/how-much-does-on-site-ai-training-cost-uk-2026) (£495–£5,500 tiers), [ReadyToday](https://readytoday.co.uk/ai-training) (£375–£850 plus £5.99/person/month), [AIUK](https://ai-uk.io/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/AIUK-Workshop-Catalogue.pdf) (£660/person, 40% off for 5+). The broader **UK online education and training market is ~£4.7 billion (2024)** (IBISWorld, cited in brief).

### 4.8 Total addressable institutional spend

**Author's synthesis:** The directly contestable, AI-relevant institutional pools are roughly: ring-fenced digital-inclusion funds **~£24m/yr**; a capturable digital/AI slice of the **£1.455bn** AEB (low tens of millions via provider routes); telecom/bank CSR (tens of millions across BT, Barclays, VMO2, Three combined); and the employer AI-training market (the largest and fastest-growing, underpinned by the **10-million-worker target** and **£68bn private investment**). Realistically a new 2-person entrant can only access a **low-six-to-seven-figure** sliver of this in early years — the constraint is access and procurement, not headline budget size.

## 7. Unit Economics

All figures in this section are the author's estimates, anchored to the pricing benchmarks in Section 4 ([LogicRoad](https://logicroad.co.uk/blog/how-much-does-on-site-ai-training-cost-uk-2026), [ReadyToday](https://readytoday.co.uk/ai-training), [Employer Skills Survey](https://learnexperts.ai/blog/how-much-do-companies-spend-on-training-per-employee/), [TechCares](https://techcares.co.uk/Home/price)) and consumer price ceilings (Section 4.6).

### 7.1 Top 5 product concepts — detailed economics

**1. Small Business AI Adoption Clinic (B2B half-day)**
- Price per unit: **£980–£1,950 per session** (15–30 attendees).
- CAC: **£80–£250** (chamber-of-commerce events, referral, LinkedIn).
- Conversion (lead → booking): **~10–15%**.
- Delivery cost: **~£250–£400** (trainer day-rate fraction + materials + travel).
- Gross margin: **~70–80%**.
- Repeat/retention: **Medium** — follow-on advanced sessions and per-seat subscription add-ons.
- Break-even: **~2–3 sessions/month** covers a lean overhead base.
- Monthly profit at 20 sessions/month (aspirational, needs >2 trainers): **~£18,000–£28,000 gross** before overhead.
- Scale bottleneck: **trainer supply and sales throughput**; mitigated by train-the-trainer.

**2. AI Scam Safety Workshop (institution-paid)**
- Price: **£800–£1,800 per cohort** (council/bank/housing-association funded) or 0–£50/head consumer.
- CAC: **£20–£55** (institutional sales effort dominant).
- Conversion: **~20%** once a sponsor relationship exists.
- Delivery cost: **~£200–£350** per cohort.
- Gross margin: **~55–75%**.
- Retention: **High at institution level** (annual programmes), low at individual level.
- Break-even: **~3–4 cohorts/month**.
- Monthly profit at 20 cohorts/month: **~£10,000–£20,000 gross**.
- Scale bottleneck: **sponsor pipeline**; demand validated by **1.9m older people scammed, £7.4bn lost** ([Independent Age](https://www.independentage.org/sites/default/files/2024-10/The_hidden_cost_of_scams_Independent_Age.pdf)).

**3. AI Help Desk for Families (subscription)**
- Price: **£15–£40/month** per supported relative.
- CAC: **£25–£60** (Facebook/carer communities, family referral).
- Conversion (trial → paid): **~15–25%**.
- Delivery cost: **~£6–£15/month** at moderate contact volume.
- Gross margin: **~55–75%**.
- Retention: **Medium** — sticky while the relative is supported; churn on bereavement/care transition.
- Break-even: **~150–250 subscribers** covers a lean two-person base.
- Monthly profit at 20 net new/month (cumulative matters): modest early, compounding; **~£3,000–£7,000 gross at ~400 subs**.
- Scale bottleneck: **support labour**; mitigated by AI-assisted triage and tiered SLAs.

**4. Train-the-Trainer Package (licensing)**
- Price: **£1,500–£6,000 per organisation** + optional annual renewal.
- CAC: **sales effort** (warm via delivered pilots).
- Conversion: **~20–30%** of orgs that have seen the curriculum work.
- Delivery cost: **~£300–£700** (onboarding + materials).
- Gross margin: **~70–90%**.
- Retention: **High** (annual updates, certification renewals).
- Break-even: **~1–2 licences/month**.
- Monthly profit at 20/month (mature stage): **~£25,000–£90,000 gross**.
- Scale bottleneck: **requires a proven curriculum and brand first** — a year-2+ product.

**5. Employer-Sponsored AI Onboarding for frontline staff (B2B per-seat)**
- Price: **£395–£795 per person** ([LogicRoad](https://logicroad.co.uk/blog/how-much-does-on-site-ai-training-cost-uk-2026)).
- CAC: **£100–£300** per closed account (then amortised over seats).
- Conversion: **~10%** of qualified leads.
- Delivery cost: **~£80–£200/person** at cohort scale.
- Gross margin: **~65–80%**.
- Retention: **Medium–high** (annual refreshes; new-hire pipeline).
- Break-even: **~25–40 seats/month**.
- Monthly profit at 20 seats/month: **~£5,000–£10,000 gross**; far higher at enterprise cohort sizes.
- Scale bottleneck: **enterprise sales cycle**; tailwind from **10m-worker upskilling target and £68bn investment** ([AI Opportunities Action Plan](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-opportunities-action-plan-dashboard/ai-opportunities-action-plan-infographic-accessible)).

## 9. Pros and Cons

### Material pros

1. **Enormous, durable need.** **7.9 million adults lack Foundation digital skills**, **10.2 million lack digital skills overall**, and **22.6 million think their skills need improving** ([Lloyds CDI](https://www.lloydsbankinggroup.com/assets/pdfs/media/consumer-digital-index/2024-consumer-digital-index-report.pdf)). This is not a fad.
2. **The gap is widening, not closing.** AI adoption jumped to **54% of adults from 31% in 2024** ([Ofcom via BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cgqk718l4neo)) while **only 20% of boomers have used GenAI** ([Deloitte](https://www.deloitte.com/content/dam/assets-zone2/uk/en/docs/industries/technology-media-telecommunications/2025/digital-consumer-trends-uk-edition-2025.pdf)) — the divide deepens as the mainstream races ahead.
3. **Strong policy tailwind and public money.** The **10-million-worker upskilling target**, **£68bn private investment** ([AI Opportunities Action Plan](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-opportunities-action-plan-dashboard/ai-opportunities-action-plan-infographic-accessible)), and dedicated inclusion funds create buyers and legitimacy.
4. **A clear competitive gap.** Incumbents are free but **not AI-specific** (Good Things, Barclays, BT/AbilityNet, Age UK); paid B2B players ignore vulnerable consumers entirely (Section 5).
5. **A visceral, monetisable wedge exists.** Scam fear is acute — **1.9m older people scammed, £7.4bn lost** ([Independent Age](https://www.independentage.org/sites/default/files/2024-10/The_hidden_cost_of_scams_Independent_Age.pdf)); **50% of over-60s fear browsing** ([Age UK London](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/london/about-us/news/articles/2023/online-scams/)).
6. **Willing third-party payers.** Families, employers (**~£1,700/employee** training spend, [Employer Skills Survey](https://learnexperts.ai/blog/how-much-do-companies-spend-on-training-per-employee/)), councils, banks and telecoms can pay where the user cannot.
7. **High-margin, scalable models available.** Train-the-trainer and licensing offer **70–90% gross margins** with very high scalability (Section 7).
8. **Proven delivery blueprints.** BT/AbilityNet's **95% satisfaction across 5,063 people** ([AbilityNet](https://abilitynet.org.uk/sites/abilitynet.org.uk/files/BT-Digital-Skills-Impact-Report_Phase1-Full.pdf)) shows the model works and can be adapted with an AI layer.
9. **Strong, quantified social-value narrative** for grant capture: **£13.7bn (life) and £23bn (work)** value of digital skills, **9.7:1 benefit ratio** ([Good Things](https://www.goodthingsfoundation.org/policy-and-research/research-and-evidence/research-2024/digital-nation); [techUK study](https://www.techskills.org/globalassets/pdfs/research-2015/economicimpactofdigitalskillsandinclusionintheuk_nov15.pdf)).

### Material cons

1. **The neediest users cannot or will not pay.** The Very Low group is **54% aged 70+ and 41% earning under £20,000** ([Lloyds CDI](https://www.lloydsbankinggroup.com/assets/pdfs/media/consumer-digital-index/2024-consumer-digital-index-report.pdf)) — minimal disposable income and willingness.
2. **Active disinterest, not just ignorance.** **38% are "not interested" and 35% "don't need" GenAI** ([Ofcom via The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/11/29/ofcom_online_nation/)); among 70+, **only 26% think AI is useful** ([ONS attitudes survey, cited in brief]). Selling against indifference is brutal.
3. **A near-zero price ceiling set by free/charitable rivals.** Free incumbents plus **£20/session** charitable rates ([Age Concern Epsom](https://ageconcernepsom.org.uk/our-services/it-support/)) cap any consumer pricing.
4. **High support burden and safeguarding load.** Vulnerable, low-confidence, sometimes frail users demand high-touch service that erodes margin and adds duty-of-care risk.
5. **Slow, lumpy institutional revenue.** Council/grant procurement is cyclical and competitive; the entire ring-fenced inclusion pot is **only ~£24m** (Section 4.1) and heavily contested by entrenched players.
6. **Crowded, trusted incumbents with scale and free pricing** — out-competing on price is impossible; differentiation must be on AI-specialisation and quality.
7. **Channel paradox.** The cheapest channels (apps, QR, online) structurally exclude the target users, who **prefer face-to-face (64%)** ([Lloyds CDI](https://www.lloydsbankinggroup.com/assets/pdfs/media/consumer-digital-index/2024-consumer-digital-index-report.pdf)) — so distribution stays expensive.
8. **Reputational fragility.** A single incident of an AI hallucination harming a vulnerable person, or a scam facilitated through the service, could be existential.
9. **AI volatility.** Rapidly changing tools mean curricula and screenshots date fast, raising content-refresh cost.
10. **Founder dependence in early delivery** — a 2-person team is capacity-bound until train-the-trainer matures, capping early revenue (Section 2).

## 10. Ethical and Trust Risks

This market involves vulnerable adults, and the ethical surface area is unusually large. These risks are not peripheral — they are central to whether the business deserves to exist and whether it can survive reputationally.

**Privacy and data handling (UK GDPR).** Helping users with NHS appointments, benefits forms and bills involves special-category and financial data. Any service must minimise data capture, avoid storing user content where possible, obtain clear lawful basis and consent, and never feed personal data into third-party AI tools without explicit, informed agreement. The cohort's low confidence means **consent must be genuinely informed, not box-ticking** — many will not understand where their data goes.

**Scam and fraud risk to vulnerable users.** Paradoxically, a service designed to reduce scam harm could itself become a scam vector or be impersonated. Given **76% of scam cases originated online in 2023** and **fraud is 40%+ of all crime in England and Wales** ([Lloyds CDI](https://www.lloydsbankinggroup.com/assets/pdfs/media/consumer-digital-index/2024-consumer-digital-index-report.pdf)), and that **10% of over-60s have nobody to turn to for digital help, rising to 20% for those without children** ([Age UK London](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/london/about-us/news/articles/2023/online-scams/)), the service must hold an exceptional trust standard — verified identity, no unsolicited contact, no payment requests by phone, and active education on impersonation.

**Overdependence.** A WhatsApp/phone help desk risks fostering learned helplessness rather than capability. The ethical design goal is graduation toward independence, not permanent reliance — though this tension conflicts with subscription-revenue incentives and must be managed honestly.

**Misinformation and hallucination risk.** Generative AI errs confidently. For users who **only 18% trust AI output** ([Ofcom via The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/11/29/ofcom_online_nation/)) yet may over-trust once onboarded, hallucinations on health, benefits or legal matters could cause real harm. Mandatory guardrails: never let AI fill official forms unverified, always teach verification, and clearly mark AI output as fallible.

**Consent and safeguarding.** Working with frail or cognitively impaired adults triggers safeguarding duties. Staff and volunteers need DBS checks, safeguarding training, and clear escalation routes for suspected abuse or financial exploitation.

**Is selling AI to reluctant low-tech users exploitative?** This is the sharpest ethical question. Pushing AI onto the **38% "not interested"** ([Ofcom via The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/11/29/ofcom_online_nation/)) risks being paternalistic or predatory. The ethical line: lead with the user's own stated needs (safety, saving money, contacting family, accessing services) rather than the technology, and accept "no" gracefully. Selling AI *for its own sake* to this group is not defensible; solving their problems *with* AI is.

**Accessibility and disability considerations (Equality Act 2010).** Disabled adults are **more fraud-exposed (9.1% vs 7.4%)** ([ONS via Lloyds CDI](https://www.lloydsbankinggroup.com/assets/pdfs/media/consumer-digital-index/2024-consumer-digital-index-report.pdf)) and have specific access needs. Materials and delivery must meet accessibility standards (large print, audio, screen-reader compatibility, plain English) — the Equality Act imposes a duty to make reasonable adjustments, and AbilityNet's model demonstrates the standard.

**Reputational risk.** If AI causes harm to a vulnerable person under the service's banner, the damage is severe and possibly fatal to the business. This argues for conservative product design, strong indemnities, professional indemnity insurance, and human-in-the-loop on anything consequential.

**Regulatory considerations.** Beyond **UK GDPR** and the **Equality Act 2010**, delivery in care settings engages the **Care Act 2014** safeguarding framework and CQC-adjacent expectations. Funded delivery (AEB, Skills Bootcamps) carries audit and outcome-reporting obligations. Bank/telecom partnerships will impose their own compliance and vetting regimes.

> **Ethical bottom line:** This business is defensible only if framed as *protection and empowerment* on the user's terms, with safeguarding and data minimisation built in from day one. Framed as *selling AI to people who don't want it*, it is both commercially weak and ethically dubious.

## 11. Final Recommendation

**Primary recommended wedge:** A **scam-safety-led digital confidence programme for older and vulnerable adults, paid for by institutions (councils, housing associations, banks, telecoms) and families**, with AI introduced as a practical safety and life-admin tool, delivered through trusted in-person and phone/WhatsApp channels. Rationale: it pairs the market's strongest emotional motivator (**1.9m scammed, £7.4bn lost**, [Independent Age](https://www.independentage.org/sites/default/files/2024-10/The_hidden_cost_of_scams_Independent_Age.pdf); **50% fear browsing**, [Age UK London](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/london/about-us/news/articles/2023/online-scams/)) with the market's most willing payers (banks are the **most-trusted source on online safety at 81%**, [Lloyds CDI](https://www.lloydsbankinggroup.com/assets/pdfs/media/consumer-digital-index/2024-consumer-digital-index-report.pdf)) and sidesteps the "AI not interested" objection entirely.

**Two secondary wedges:**
1. **Small Business AI Adoption Clinics** — highest margins (**70–80%**), clear ROI story, and demand validated by **43% of SMEs having no AI plans** ([SME Taskforce](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/sme-digital-adoption-taskforce-final-report)) against a **10-million-worker national target** ([AI Opportunities Action Plan](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-opportunities-action-plan-dashboard/ai-opportunities-action-plan-infographic-accessible)).
2. **AI Help Desk / Toolkit for Family Carers** — monetises the relative who *can* pay, addresses the **10% with nobody to ask for help** ([Age UK London](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/london/about-us/news/articles/2023/online-scams/)), and is under-served by every incumbent.

**Top no-go wedge:** A **paid direct-to-consumer "AI course" app requiring the excluded user to self-onboard.** It collides with the near-zero price ceiling, free trusted rivals, active disinterest (**38% not interested**, [Ofcom via The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/11/29/ofcom_online_nation/)), and a cohort that **prefers face-to-face (64%)** and resists self-serve ([Lloyds CDI](https://www.lloydsbankinggroup.com/assets/pdfs/media/consumer-digital-index/2024-consumer-digital-index-report.pdf)). Care-home-resident-pays models are a close second no-go (frailty, safeguarding, thin margin).

**Best first paid offer:** An **AI Scam Safety + Digital Confidence cohort programme sold to a housing association or council at £800–£1,800 per cohort**, with a per-tenant funded value, delivered in-person at a trusted local venue with phone/WhatsApp follow-up. Buyer: housing association / council inclusion lead. Channel: direct B2B BD plus the **Digital Inclusion Innovation Fund** grant route (**£25k–£500k grants**, [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/digital-inclusion-innovation-fund/digital-inclusion-innovation-fund)). Rationale: institution pays, captive cohort, sticky annual renewal, strong social-value reporting for the funder.

**Best free lead magnet:** A **printed and downloadable "Spot the Scam — and use AI safely" guide** plus a free 45-minute community taster session, co-branded with a trusted local partner (library, Age UK branch, faith group). It builds trust, generates referrals, and feeds both the institutional and family-paid funnels.

**Best first partner channel:** A **housing association or a regional council** — concentrated, fundable, captive cohorts with statutory inclusion interest, far faster to contract than a national bank/telecom (which should be a year-2 target once a proof point exists).

**Minimum viable pilot:**
- **What:** Deliver 6–8 scam-safety + AI confidence cohorts (12–20 people each) plus a phone/WhatsApp follow-up line, with a printed workbook.
- **Where:** One housing association or council area (single city, e.g. a Lloyds-flagged low-skill region such as Wales or the North East, where **Foundation/Work EDS are weakest**, [Lloyds CDI](https://www.lloydsbankinggroup.com/assets/pdfs/media/consumer-digital-index/2024-consumer-digital-index-report.pdf)).
- **How long:** 12 weeks.
- **Cost:** **~£15,000–£25,000** (two facilitators part-time, materials, venue, light tooling) — author estimate; ideally grant-offset.
- **Success metrics:** ≥75% of attendees report increased confidence (BT/AbilityNet hit **3 in 4 more confident**, [AbilityNet](https://abilitynet.org.uk/sites/abilitynet.org.uk/files/BT-Digital-Skills-Impact-Report_Phase1-Full.pdf)); ≥85% satisfaction; ≥1 paid renewal or follow-on contract committed; ≥10% of attendees convert a family member to a paid help-desk subscription; demonstrable scam-avoidance anecdotes for the funder's report.

**Estimated probability of commercial success over 5 years** (author estimate; "success" = sustainable profitable business with >£500k revenue):
- **Low: ~20%** (if pursued as D2C consumer product).
- **Base: ~40%** (institution- and family-paid, disciplined wedge, lean team).
- **High: ~60%** (if a bank/telecom CSR partnership or a train-the-trainer licensing flywheel lands within 24 months).

**What evidence would change the recommendation:**
- **Upgrade** if a pilot shows institutional buyers will pay **>£1,500/cohort with annual renewal**, or a bank/telecom signs a paid AI-specific CSR partnership, or family help-desk trial-to-paid conversion exceeds **20%** with low churn.
- **Downgrade** if pilots reveal cohort no-show/attrition above ~40%, if institutional sales cycles exceed 9–12 months with low close rates, if support costs on the help desk exceed ~45% of subscription revenue, or if free incumbents (Good Things, Barclays, Age UK) launch credible AI-specific modules at scale — which would compress the whitespace identified in Section 5.

> **Closing verdict:** A worthwhile business exists here — but it is a *mission-led, institution-and-family-funded, trust-first* business, not a venture-scale consumer app. Build for the payer, frame for the user's fears and needs, lead with safety, and treat AI as the quiet engine rather than the loud product.
